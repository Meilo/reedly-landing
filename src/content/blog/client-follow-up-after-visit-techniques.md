You just spent an hour and twenty minutes with a prospect. The meeting went well. Real interest, relevant questions, a clear budget discussed. You shake hands, get back in the car, and tell yourself: "This one's going to close." Then you wait. Two days. A week. Radio silence. You send a generic email. Still nothing. The deal goes cold.

Sound familiar? The issue isn't the visit itself—it's what happens after. Most salespeople think the follow-up is just a formality. In reality, it's where deals are won or lost.

## The 2-hour window rule

Here's what nobody tells you: **the best time to follow up isn't the next day, it's within two hours of leaving the client's office.** Not with a formal summary—with a quick, personal message. A text, a voice note, even a short email sent from your phone.

Why? Because you're still fresh in their mind. The conversation just happened. Your competitor who's going to see them tomorrow hasn't shown up yet. This micro-follow-up isn't about closing—it's about staying present. Something like: "Jean, great talking earlier. I'm sending over the pricing scenario we discussed by end of day." Thirty seconds to write. Massive impact on response rates.

## Match the channel to the relationship stage

First visit? Stick to email—it's professional, expected, non-intrusive. Third meeting? Pick up the phone. If you've already spent three hours together and you're still hiding behind emails, you're signaling distance. The channel choice sends a message about where you think the relationship stands.

Voice notes work surprisingly well with clients under 50 who already text with you. It's personal without demanding immediate attention. One sales director I know closes 40% of his deals using WhatsApp voice messages for follow-ups. Not because it's trendy, but because it cuts through the inbox noise.

## Ask one specific question

Generic follow-ups die in inboxes. "Just checking in"—delete. "Any thoughts on our discussion?"—ignore. But a precise question tied to something they said? That gets answered.

Reference a specific concern they raised. "You mentioned your ops team was skeptical about implementation time—I spoke with our tech lead and we can get you a sandbox environment by Friday. Would that help you test internally?" You're not asking if they're interested. You're solving a problem they named. That's the difference between a brush-off and a conversation.

## The Friday afternoon rule

Never send important follow-ups on Friday after 2pm. Your carefully crafted email will sit in their inbox all weekend, get buried by Monday morning chaos, and die. Tuesday morning between 8am and 10am has the highest open and response rates for B2B sales emails. Thursday works too. Friday is where follow-ups go to be forgotten.

## Build the next meeting into this one

The strongest client follow-up after visit happens before you leave the room. End every meeting by scheduling the next touchpoint. Not "I'll send you something and we'll reconnect"—that's a dead end. "How about we regroup on the 18th to review the proposal with your CFO? Does 10am work?" Get it in both calendars before you walk out.

When the next step is already scheduled, your follow-up has a clear purpose: preparing for that next meeting. It's no longer "checking in." It's "here's what we need to cover on Thursday." Completely different dynamic.

The sale doesn't happen in the meeting room. It happens in the space between meetings. Master post-meeting follow-up, and you'll stop losing deals you thought you had.
